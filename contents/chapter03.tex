\chapter{Proposed Methods}

\section{Overall Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/method_pipline}
    \caption{Overview of the proposed four-stage assessment pipeline.}
    \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

The proposed system is a four-stage pipeline for automated PD assessment from hand-rotation video, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}. A raw video is first processed by MediaPipe Hands to extract a temporal sequence of 21 three-dimensional keypoint coordinates per hand. Three families of kinematic descriptors---\emph{frequency}, \emph{clarity}, and \emph{amplitude}---are then computed from the resulting skeletal time series. The high-dimensional feature vector is subsequently either reduced by one of three feature-selection strategies for the conventional classifiers, or organised as a joint-wise feature graph for the feature-based adaptive graph convolutional network (AGCN-style) model. The final classifier outputs a binary PD-versus-healthy label.

The remainder of this chapter describes each stage in detail. Section~\ref{sec:fe} covers feature extraction, Section~\ref{sec:fs} describes the three feature-selection methods, and Section~\ref{sec:joint_corr} presents the joint-correlation module and the feature-based AGCN-style branch.

\section{Feature Extraction}
\label{sec:fe}

Feature extraction is applied independently to every combination of hand, joint, and spatial axis. Both hands are processed, each containing 21 joints recorded along 3 spatial axes, yielding $2 \times 21 \times 3 = 126$ independent trajectories per subject. For each trajectory, frequency, clarity, and four amplitude representations are computed as described in the following subsections.

\subsection{Skeleton Sequence Preprocessing}
\label{sec:skeleton_preprocess}

The skeleton extraction code processes each video frame sequentially with MediaPipe Hands and stores the resulting sequence in the original frame order. If a hand is detected in a frame, all 21 MediaPipe landmarks are saved as normalised $(x,y,z)$ coordinates. If MediaPipe fails to detect a hand in an intermediate frame, the frame is retained but represented by an all-zero $21 \times 3$ landmark array, preserving the temporal length of the original video. Videos with no valid hand detections are discarded. For compatibility with older skeleton files containing only two-dimensional coordinates, a zero-valued $z$ coordinate is appended before feature extraction.

No explicit per-video frame-rate resampling is applied in the hand-crafted feature pipeline. The frequency descriptor is computed from the first positive autocorrelation peak using a nominal sampling rate of 60 frames per second; therefore, temporal alignment is performed in frame units rather than by interpolating all recordings onto a shared time grid. Variable recording lengths are handled by summary statistics over each available trajectory. The AGCN-style experiment uses the same summary-feature representation rather than the raw frame sequence: the 1,764-dimensional bilateral feature vector is reshaped into a 42-node graph, where each node corresponds to one MediaPipe hand joint and stores the descriptors associated with that joint.

\subsection{Frequency and Clarity}
\label{sec:fe1}

Two scalar descriptors---\emph{frequency} and \emph{clarity}---are derived from the autocorrelation of each joint-axis trajectory. Clinically, PD patients tend to perform the pronation-supination task at a slower pace and with irregular rhythm; both descriptors are therefore expected to carry strong discriminative signals.

Before autocorrelation is computed, each trajectory is detrended to remove low-frequency drift caused by gradual hand-position changes or camera motion. A polynomial is fit to the signal and subtracted, yielding a zero-mean detrended waveform, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wave_detrend}.

Let $r(\tau)$ denote the autocorrelation of the detrended signal, and let $\tau_{\mathrm{peak}}$ be the lag, measured in frames, of the first positive-lag peak. The \emph{frequency} descriptor is computed as $f_{\mathrm{rot}} = f_s / \tau_{\mathrm{peak}}$, where $f_s=60$ frames per second is the nominal sampling rate used in the implementation; $\tau_{\mathrm{peak}}$ therefore corresponds to the dominant rotation period. The \emph{clarity} descriptor is the normalised peak height $r(\tau_{\mathrm{peak}})/r(0)$; values near one indicate high periodicity while low values reflect irregular rhythm. Fig.~\ref{fig:autocorr} shows a representative autocorrelation with the peak lag annotated.

\subsection{Amplitude Representations}
\label{sec:fe2}

PD patients typically exhibit progressive amplitude reduction and cycle-to-cycle fluctuation within a single recording trial. Four complementary representations are computed from the detrended trajectory to capture different aspects of this phenomenon, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:amplitude_detection}.

\emph{Cycle-by-cycle amplitude} identifies all local maxima and minima in the detrended trajectory, pairs adjacent peaks and valleys, and records the absolute difference of each pair as the instantaneous one-cycle amplitude. This representation gives the most direct measure of per-cycle displacement but is sensitive to isolated noise spikes.

\emph{Rolling peak-to-peak} avoids explicit peak detection by sliding a fixed-length window along the trajectory and recording, at each position, the local maximum minus the local minimum. This coarser amplitude estimate is more robust to noise than the cycle-by-cycle approach.

\emph{Rolling RMS} computes the root-mean-square of the detrended signal within a sliding window at each frame. The squaring operation distributes any single outlier's influence across the window, making rolling RMS the most noise-robust of the four representations.

\emph{Hilbert envelope} derives instantaneous amplitude from the analytic signal. The Hilbert transform supplies the $90^{\circ}$-shifted quadrature component of the detrended waveform; the pointwise magnitude of the resulting complex analytic signal yields the instantaneous envelope. This representation tracks amplitude modulation at per-frame resolution, making it the most sensitive to rapid amplitude changes and complementary to the smoothed rolling RMS.

Before amplitude statistics are summarised, each hand trajectory is scale-normalised by the median Euclidean distance between the wrist landmark and the middle-finger MCP landmark within that recording. This subject-level normalisation reduces the effect of camera distance and hand size on displacement-based amplitude descriptors.

Feature extraction is applied to all 126 trajectories ($2~\text{hands} \times 21~\text{joints} \times 3~\text{axes}$). Each trajectory yields 2 autocorrelation features and 12 amplitude features, giving a total dimensionality of
\begin{equation}
    2 \times 21 \times 3 \times (2 + 4 \times 3) = 1764.
    \label{eq:feature_dim}
\end{equation}

\section{Feature Selection}
\label{sec:fs}

The feature dimensionality of 1,764 greatly exceeds the number of available samples in either clinical cohort, so feature selection is applied to mitigate overfitting and improve interpretability. Three methods are evaluated. Each method independently ranks all 1,764 features according to its own criterion and retains the top 10 features for downstream classification.

\emph{ANOVA F-value selection} ranks features by between-class to within-class variance ratio and retains the 10 features with the highest F-values. The method is computationally efficient but treats each feature independently and cannot capture inter-feature correlations.

\emph{L1-regularised logistic regression} imposes an L1 penalty that shrinks uninformative feature coefficients to zero. The 10 features with the largest absolute coefficients after regularisation are retained, implicitly accounting for linear feature interactions.

\emph{XGBoost gain-based importance} trains an XGBoost model and ranks features by average gain across all tree splits, reflecting non-linear and combinatorial relationships. The top 10 features by gain are selected; the resulting ranking is less directly interpretable than a linear coefficient. Table~\ref{tab:fs_methods_comparison} summarises the three methods.

\section{Joint-Correlation Information}
\label{sec:joint_corr}

Some joint pairs are anatomically unconnected yet may exhibit tight motion coupling---or abnormal decoupling---under PD. Two approaches are evaluated to encode such inter-joint relationships.

\subsection{Pairwise Correlation Features}
\label{sec:joint_corr_corr}

A per-patient adjacency matrix $\mathbf{A}^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^{42 \times 42}$ is constructed from the kinematic feature vectors, covering all 21 joints of the left hand and 21 joints of the right hand. Recall from Section~\ref{sec:fe3} that each joint contributes $3 \text{ axes} \times (2 + 4 \times 3) = 42$ scalar features; the 1,764-dimensional feature vector is therefore partitioned into 42 equal-length blocks of length $d = 42$, one per joint. Let $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the feature block of joint $i$; stacking these blocks yields a data matrix $\mathbf{X}^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^{42 \times d}$ for patient $n$.

The coupling strength between joint $i$ and joint $j$ is quantified by the Pearson correlation coefficient between their $d$-dimensional feature vectors $\mathbf{x}_i^{(n)}$ and $\mathbf{x}_j^{(n)}$:
\begin{equation}
    C_{ij}^{(n)} =
    \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}
          \!\left(x_{ik}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{i}^{(n)}\right)
          \!\left(x_{jk}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{j}^{(n)}\right)}
         {\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}
                \!\left(x_{ik}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{i}^{(n)}\right)^{2}}
          \;\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}
                  \!\left(x_{jk}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{j}^{(n)}\right)^{2}}},
    \label{eq:corr}
\end{equation}
where $x_{ik}^{(n)}$ is the $k$-th feature of joint $i$ for patient $n$ and $\bar{x}_{i}^{(n)}$ is its mean over the $d$ features. $C_{ij}^{(n)} \in [-1, 1]$, with $+1$ indicating perfect co-movement and $-1$ indicating perfect anti-movement between the two joints. Because graph adjacency weights are required to be non-negative, the Pearson correlation is remapped to $[0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
    A_{ij}^{(n)} = \frac{C_{ij}^{(n)} + 1}{2}, \qquad A_{ii}^{(n)} = 0.
    \label{eq:adj}
\end{equation}
The resulting matrix $\mathbf{A}^{(n)}$ is symmetric with zero diagonal, encoding the functional coupling strength between every joint pair for patient $n$. The upper-triangular entries of $\mathbf{A}^{(n)}$ are flattened and appended to the bilateral kinematic feature vector for the experiments reported in Section~\ref{sec:exp4}.

\subsection{Adaptive Graph Convolutional Network}
\label{sec:joint_corr_agcn}

The feature-based AGCN-style model (reviewed conceptually in Section~\ref{sec:rw_agcn}) treats the bilateral feature vector as a graph over the 42 hand joints. Each joint node receives its corresponding kinematic descriptors as node features. Starting from a fixed hand-skeleton adjacency prior, the model learns an adaptive residual graph from the node features using a Mahalanobis distance and Gaussian kernel, enabling the network to capture joint relationships across both hands simultaneously. Unlike the pairwise correlation descriptor, this learned graph can express higher-order, jointly-defined inter-joint patterns.

These two approaches form a deliberate contrast: pairwise correlation provides an interpretable hand-crafted baseline restricted to explicit bilateral joint interactions, while the AGCN-style model learns a global, data-driven relational structure from the same feature space. Their respective contributions are evaluated empirically in Experiments~4 and~5 of Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.
