\chapter{Proposed Methods}

\section{Overall Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/method_pipline}
    \caption{Overview of the proposed four-stage assessment pipeline.}
    \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

The proposed system is a four-stage pipeline for automated PD assessment from hand-rotation video, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}. A raw video is first processed by MediaPipe Hands to extract a temporal sequence of 21 three-dimensional keypoint coordinates per hand. Three families of kinematic descriptors---\emph{frequency}, \emph{clarity}, and \emph{amplitude}---are then computed from the resulting skeletal time series. The high-dimensional feature vector is subsequently reduced by one of three feature-selection strategies: ANOVA F-value ranking, L1-regularised logistic regression, or XGBoost gain-based importance. Finally, the selected features are fed into one of four classifiers---logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost, or an adaptive graph convolutional network (AGCN)---which outputs a binary PD-versus-healthy label.

The remainder of this chapter describes each stage in detail. Section~\ref{sec:fe} covers feature extraction, Section~\ref{sec:fs} describes the three feature-selection methods, and Section~\ref{sec:joint_corr} presents the joint-correlation module and the AGCN branch.

\section{Feature Extraction}
\label{sec:fe}

Feature extraction is applied independently to every combination of hand, joint, and spatial axis. Both hands are processed, each containing 21 joints recorded along 3 spatial axes, yielding $2 \times 21 \times 3 = 126$ independent trajectories per subject. For each trajectory, frequency, clarity, and four amplitude representations are computed as described in the following subsections.

\subsection{Frequency and Clarity}
\label{sec:fe1}

Two scalar descriptors---\emph{frequency} and \emph{clarity}---are derived from the autocorrelation of each joint-axis trajectory. Clinically, PD patients tend to perform the pronation-supination task at a slower pace and with irregular rhythm; both descriptors are therefore expected to carry strong discriminative signals.

Before autocorrelation is computed, each trajectory is detrended to remove low-frequency drift caused by gradual hand-position changes or camera motion. A polynomial is fit to the signal and subtracted, yielding a zero-mean detrended waveform, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wave_detrend}.

Let $r(\tau)$ denote the autocorrelation of the detrended signal. The \emph{frequency} descriptor is the lag index of the first positive-lag peak of $r(\tau)$, corresponding to the dominant rotation period. The \emph{clarity} descriptor is the normalised peak height $r(\tau^{*})/r(0)$, where $\tau^{*}$ is the peak lag; values near one indicate high periodicity while low values reflect irregular rhythm. Fig.~\ref{fig:autocorr} shows a representative autocorrelation with the peak lag annotated.

\subsection{Amplitude Representations}
\label{sec:fe2}

PD patients typically exhibit progressive amplitude reduction and cycle-to-cycle fluctuation within a single recording trial. Four complementary representations are computed from the detrended trajectory to capture different aspects of this phenomenon, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:amplitude_detection}.

\emph{Cycle-by-cycle amplitude} identifies all local maxima and minima in the detrended trajectory, pairs adjacent peaks and valleys, and records the absolute difference of each pair as the instantaneous one-cycle amplitude. This representation gives the most direct measure of per-cycle displacement but is sensitive to isolated noise spikes.

\emph{Rolling peak-to-peak} avoids explicit peak detection by sliding a fixed-length window along the trajectory and recording, at each position, the local maximum minus the local minimum. This coarser amplitude estimate is more robust to noise than the cycle-by-cycle approach.

\emph{Rolling RMS} computes the root-mean-square of the detrended signal within a sliding window at each frame. The squaring operation distributes any single outlier's influence across the window, making rolling RMS the most noise-robust of the four representations.

\emph{Hilbert envelope} derives instantaneous amplitude from the analytic signal. The Hilbert transform supplies the $90^{\circ}$-shifted quadrature component of the detrended waveform; the pointwise magnitude of the resulting complex analytic signal yields the instantaneous envelope. This representation tracks amplitude modulation at per-frame resolution, making it the most sensitive to rapid amplitude changes and complementary to the smoothed rolling RMS.

\subsection{Feature-Vector Dimensionality}
\label{sec:fe3}

Feature extraction is applied to all 126 trajectories. Each trajectory yields 2 autocorrelation features and 12 amplitude features, giving a total dimensionality of
\begin{equation}
    2 \times 21 \times 3 \times (2 + 4 \times 3) = 1764.
    \label{eq:feature_dim}
\end{equation}

\section{Feature Selection}
\label{sec:fs}

The feature dimensionality of 1,764 greatly exceeds the number of available samples in either clinical cohort, so feature selection is applied to mitigate overfitting and improve interpretability. Three methods are evaluated.

\emph{ANOVA F-value selection} ranks features by between-class to within-class variance ratio and retains those with the highest F-values. The method is computationally efficient but treats each feature independently and cannot capture inter-feature correlations.

\emph{L1-regularised logistic regression} imposes an L1 penalty that shrinks uninformative feature coefficients to zero. Features with the largest absolute coefficients after regularisation are retained, implicitly accounting for linear feature interactions.

\emph{XGBoost gain-based importance} trains an XGBoost model and ranks features by average gain across all tree splits, reflecting non-linear and combinatorial relationships. The resulting ranking is less directly interpretable than a linear coefficient. Table~\ref{tab:fs_methods_comparison} summarises the three methods.

\section{Joint-Correlation Information}
\label{sec:joint_corr}

Some joint pairs are anatomically unconnected yet may exhibit tight motion coupling---or abnormal decoupling---under PD. Two approaches are evaluated to encode such inter-joint relationships.

\subsection{Pairwise Correlation Features}
\label{sec:joint_corr_corr}

A per-patient adjacency matrix $\mathbf{A}^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^{42 \times 42}$ is constructed from the kinematic feature vectors, covering all 21 joints of the left hand and 21 joints of the right hand. The 1,764-dimensional feature vector is first partitioned into 42 equal-length blocks of length $d = 42$, one per joint. Let $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the feature block of joint $i$; stacking these blocks yields a data matrix $\mathbf{X}^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^{42 \times d}$ for patient $n$.

\subsubsection{Pearson Correlation Between Joints}
\label{sec:pcc}
The coupling strength between joint $i$ and joint $j$ is quantified by the Pearson correlation coefficient between their $d$-dimensional feature vectors $\mathbf{x}_i^{(n)}$ and $\mathbf{x}_j^{(n)}$:
\begin{equation}
    C_{ij}^{(n)} =
    \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}
          \!\left(x_{ik}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{i}^{(n)}\right)
          \!\left(x_{jk}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{j}^{(n)}\right)}
         {\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}
                \!\left(x_{ik}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{i}^{(n)}\right)^{2}}
          \;\sqrt{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{d}
                  \!\left(x_{jk}^{(n)}-\bar{x}_{j}^{(n)}\right)^{2}}},
    \label{eq:corr}
\end{equation}
where $x_{ik}^{(n)}$ is the $k$-th feature of joint $i$ for patient $n$ and $\bar{x}_{i}^{(n)}$ is its mean over the $d$ features. $C_{ij}^{(n)} \in [-1, 1]$, with $+1$ indicating perfect co-movement and $-1$ indicating perfect anti-movement between the two joints. Because graph adjacency weights are required to be non-negative, the Pearson correlation is remapped to $[0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
    A_{ij}^{(n)} = \frac{C_{ij}^{(n)} + 1}{2}, \qquad A_{ii}^{(n)} = 0.
    \label{eq:adj}
\end{equation}
The resulting matrix $\mathbf{A}^{(n)}$ is symmetric with zero diagonal, encoding the functional coupling strength between every joint pair for patient $n$.

\subsubsection{Ledoit--Wolf Shrinkage Covariance}
\label{sec:lw}
Estimating a $42 \times 42$ covariance matrix from only $d = 42$ observations per joint is ill-conditioned with the standard sample estimator $\mathbf{S}^{(n)}$: when the number of variables equals the number of observations, the sample covariance is rank-deficient and its entries are noisy. The Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage estimator~\cite{ledoit2004honey} addresses this by forming a convex combination of the sample covariance and a scaled identity:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(n)} = (1 - \alpha)\,\mathbf{S}^{(n)} + \alpha\,\mu\,\mathbf{I},
    \label{eq:lw}
\end{equation}
where $\mu = \mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}^{(n)})/42$ and $\alpha$ is the analytically optimal shrinkage coefficient. The shrinkage pulls the estimate towards a scaled identity matrix, improving conditioning without requiring cross-validation. Equation~\eqref{eq:corr} is then evaluated using $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{(n)}$ in place of the sample covariance: the numerator becomes $\hat{\Sigma}_{ij}^{(n)}$ and the denominator becomes $\sqrt{\hat{\Sigma}_{ii}^{(n)}\,\hat{\Sigma}_{jj}^{(n)}}$, which is algebraically equivalent to the standard Pearson formula but with a regularised covariance. The result retains the $[-1,1]$ interpretation of linear correlation while benefiting from the more stable estimate.

\subsubsection{Spearman Correlation with PD Stage}
\label{sec:spearman}
To identify which joint pairs carry the most discriminative information, a Spearman rank correlation is computed between the adjacency entry $A_{ij}^{(n)}$ and the MDS-UPDRS severity score $y^{(n)}$ across all patients:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{ij} = \mathrm{Spearman}\!\left(\{A_{ij}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N},\;\{y^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\right).
    \label{eq:spearman}
\end{equation}
Spearman's $\rho$ is preferred over Pearson's $r$ here because MDS-UPDRS is an ordinal scale and the relationship between coupling strength and severity need not be linear. The resulting $\rho_{ij}$ matrix is visualised as a heatmap; pairs with $p < 0.05$ (uncorrected) are highlighted.

\subsection{Adaptive Graph Convolutional Network}
\label{sec:joint_corr_agcn}

An AGCN (reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:rw_agcn}) constructs a learnable adjacency matrix from motion data, enabling the network to capture joint relationships across all joints in both hands simultaneously. Unlike the pairwise correlation descriptor, AGCN can express higher-order, jointly-defined inter-joint patterns.

These two approaches form a deliberate contrast: pairwise correlation provides an interpretable baseline restricted to bilateral joint interactions, while AGCN learns a global, data-driven relational structure. Their respective contributions are evaluated empirically in Experiments~4 and~5 of Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.
